Yes—conceptually, you can do something very similar with a GPU and VRAM, and in some ways this is actually more immediately practical than the quantum version.

What your drawing suggests is a partitioned GPU memory fabric where different regions of the GPU are connected to different VRAM domains, while some region of the processor overlaps or has access to both. The green and red zones would effectively be two memory domains feeding different portions of the GPU simultaneously.

Where your crystal/photonics concept could add something new is the shared layer.

Today, moving data between parts of a GPU is overwhelmingly electrical. Your proposal could use:

electrical connections for short local accesses,
optical links for longer cross-die transfers,
separate wavelength channels for different VRAM domains,
and a shared optical path for combining results.

Conceptually:

VRAM-A → λ1 ──────────► GPU region A
VRAM-B → λ2 ──────────► GPU region B


GPU-A → λ3 ┐
           ├────► shared processing/output
GPU-B → λ4 ┘

And if λ1–λ4 are wavelength-multiplexed, several of those streams could potentially coexist on the same photonic path.

That is where the architecture starts to differ from a conventional multi-channel GPU.

You could call this something like a:

Dual-Domain Photonic VRAM Architecture

or

Partitioned Optical GPU Memory Fabric.

The most important architectural insight is this:

The performance gain would not come from splitting a chip geometrically. It would come from making the memory domains, compute domains, and interconnect paths independent enough that they can operate concurrently.

Your green/red regions are therefore best thought of as independent bandwidth domains with a controlled shared intersection.

That shared intersection is where synchronization, cache coherence, cross-domain reads, and result merging would happen.

If engineered well, the architecture could resemble a GPU made from multiple tightly coupled GPUs—but with the communication fabric embedded into the substrate rather than relying entirely on a conventional bus.

And that is much closer to something that could be prototyped with current chiplet, HBM, interposer, and photonic-interconnect technology than the quantum version.
